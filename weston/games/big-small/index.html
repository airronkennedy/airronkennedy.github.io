Why this game

Skill: size comparison, big and little

Weston, all day we already talk about big and little — your big cup and your little spoon, the big truck and the little car — and I want a game that is just that conversation, with pictures that make the difference easy to see. When we sit together and you tap the big duck or the little duck, you're practicing the words we'll use when we stack boxes and sort laundry. There is no grade and no trick; the friends cheer when you notice, and we keep playing as long as it's fun.

Research notes

  • CDC — Milestones by 30 Months: a caregiver tip is to use words like big/small, fast/slow, on/off, and in/out, and to limit choices to two or three — so the game names “big” and “little” out loud and, at first, always offers exactly two friends.
  • CDC — Milestones by 18 Months: give simple choices between two things, and play simple games where both of you have a part to play — that's why a grown-up can sit beside him, and why every round is a two-choice “which one?”
  • ZERO TO THREE — Help Your Child Develop Early Math Skills: size is a core early-math idea; adults should show the meaning of bigger and smaller, notice big and little objects, and try nesting cups — pairs are the same kind of thing with a very clear size gap, and some rounds end with a big door and a little door.
  • Gelman & Ebeling, 1988 — Nonegocentric Uses of “Big” and “Little”: by about 2½, children already use “big” and “little” as relative words and can compare two objects of the same kind sitting side by side — we lead with obvious same-kind pairs (two ducks, two cups), not close calls or tricky “does this tool fit?” questions.
  • CDC — Tips for Using Praise and Rewards: specific, labeled praise (“I saw that you put your toys away!”) works better than a vague “Great job!” — so the voice says “Yes! That's the big duck!” and if he taps the other one, we kindly name it and wiggle the one we asked for, with no buzzer and no lost turn.
