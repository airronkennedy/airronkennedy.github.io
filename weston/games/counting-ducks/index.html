Why this game

Skill: counting 1–3, early number sense

Weston, I made Counting Ducks so we can sit together and count a little pond of friends — one duck, two ducks, three ducks — the same way we already count your shoes and your snacks. Your brain is already starting to see a tiny group and know how many are there, even before you can say every number out loud, and I want those first number words to feel like a game we play, not a lesson. When the ducks quack and a star flies into the jar, we'll take that same one, two, three into the bathtub and the park.

Research notes

  • Starkey & Cooper, 1980 — Perception of Numbers by Human Infants: a process called subitizing, already present in 2-year-olds, lets children see a small group and know how many are there, even before they can count out loud — so the default game only ever shows 1, 2, or 3 ducks, all on screen at once.
  • ZERO TO THREE — Counting as You Go: when you see groups of 1, 2, or 3, point to each and count aloud, and start with just 1 if needed — the voice counts each duck with a bounce, and rounds begin with one duck more often, then two, then three.
  • ZERO TO THREE — Ones and Twos: toddlers practice recognizing a set of 1 and a set of 2 with time and play — after a count we name the whole set (“Two ducks!”) so the number words stick to the group, not just the pointing.
  • CDC — Milestones by 2 Years: at 2, many children point to pictures when asked, try buttons that make something happen, and play with more than one toy at a time; screen play should be with an adult present — so every duck is a huge button that quacks, and the game is meant to be played together.
  • Kirkorian, Choi & Pempek, 2016 — Word learning from touchscreens: the youngest 2-year-olds learned more when they had to touch the actual thing that mattered, not “tap anywhere to continue” — so counting taps land on the ducks themselves, and pond decorations ripple but are not the lesson.
